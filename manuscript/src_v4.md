%% TITLE
A heart–brain MRI stage and risk of cardiovascular and cerebrovascular events beyond SCORE2: a prospective analysis of UK Biobank

%% SUMMARY_Background
Cardiac and cerebrovascular disease often coexist, yet risk assessment treats the heart and brain separately. We inferred the order in which subclinical heart and brain abnormality accumulates and tested whether its stage predicts vascular events beyond SCORE2.
%% SUMMARY_Methods
In UK Biobank, we applied Subtype and Stage Inference (SuStaIn) to age-normed and sex-normed z scores of three cardiac MRI, four brain MRI, and three plasma proteomic markers in 3770 participants imaged in 2014–20 (derivation sample), and applied an MRI-only model, without refitting, to 23 974 independent participants. The primary outcome was a post-imaging composite of stroke, transient ischaemic attack, ischaemic heart disease, heart failure, and atrial fibrillation.
%% SUMMARY_Findings
Two subtypes were identified. In all subtypes and models, the first cardiac event preceded the first brain event (posterior probability ≥0·95), reproduced by de novo refitting in {rep_n} independent participants. Cardiac abnormality co-occurred mainly with white-matter abnormality. Over a median {fu_med} and {ind_fu_med} years, {hbve_ev} derivation and {ind_hbve_ev} independent participants had a primary event; the adjusted hazard ratio per 10-percentage-point stage increase was {hbve_m2_full} and {ind_hbve_num}, respectively. Adding stage to SCORE2 modestly increased the C-index ({c0_hbve} to {c1_hbve}; independent sample {ind_c0} to {ind_c1}). At high or very high SCORE2 risk, independent-sample event rates were {is2hi_g0} per 1000 person-years at stage 0 and {is2hi_g3} at stage 3 or higher.
%% SUMMARY_Interpretation
Subclinical abnormality appears to accumulate heart first, mainly alongside white-matter change, and its stage stratifies vascular risk beyond SCORE2. Discrimination gains were modest, similar to those of simpler marker summaries; the stage is an interpretable summary for people already having cardiac and brain MRI, not a screening tool.

%% RIC_Evidence before this study
We searched PubMed for articles published between Jan 1, 2000, and Oct 20, 2026, without language restrictions, using four searches: data-driven progression models ("Subtype and Stage Inference", "event-based model", or "disease progression model") combined with cardiac and brain terms; cardiac MRI combined with brain MRI or white matter hyperintensities and incident or prospective outcomes; multi-organ or heart–brain ageing or staging combined with stroke, cardiovascular disease, or heart failure; and SCORE2 combined with imaging and incremental prediction. The searches returned 204, 10, 42, and 23 records. Population studies, mostly cross-sectional analyses of UK Biobank, have shown that left ventricular structure and function and aortic stiffness are associated with white matter hyperintensities, brain volume, and cognition, and multi-organ imaging and proteomic ageing measures predict chronic disease. Progression models such as SuStaIn have described the order in which abnormality appears in neurodegenerative and respiratory disease. We found no study that inferred the order in which abnormality accumulates across the heart and brain jointly, or that tested whether a stage derived from such an order adds prognostic information to a guideline cardiovascular risk score.

%% RIC_Added value of this study
In every subtype and model, cardiac abnormality preceded brain abnormality, and the same subtypes and order were recovered when the model was refitted de novo in independent participants; in nearly 24 000 independent participants cardiac abnormality was coupled with white-matter rather than grey-matter injury. The heart–brain stage was associated with stroke, heart failure, atrial fibrillation, and cardiovascular death after imaging, independently of conventional risk factors, in the derivation sample and in nearly 24 000 independent participants in whom the model was applied without refitting. The stage modestly improved discrimination beyond SCORE2 and separated risk within SCORE2 categories. An organ-concordant analysis showed that isolated brain abnormality was associated mainly with cerebrovascular disease before imaging, whereas cardiac abnormality was associated with stroke after imaging.

%% RIC_Implications of all the available evidence
Together with previous work, these findings support viewing the heart and brain as one vascular system in which cardiac abnormality is an early, measurable stage. In people who undergo both cardiac and brain MRI, a combined heart–brain stage could help to identify those whose risk is underestimated by SCORE2 and who might benefit from intensified prevention. Before clinical use, the stage needs validation in other cohorts with longer follow-up, and should be compared with simpler imaging summaries, which discriminated as well as or better than the stage in this study.

%% H1 Introduction
Stroke, ischaemic heart disease, heart failure, and atrial fibrillation share risk factors, frequently co-occur, and together account for a large proportion of global death and disability.[@GBD_stroke_2024; @Martin_AHA_2025] Prevention guidelines estimate cardiovascular risk with scores such as SCORE2 and SCORE2-OP, which combine age, sex, blood pressure, cholesterol, smoking, and diabetes but do not include measures of target-organ damage.[@Visseren_ESC_2021; @SCORE2_2021; @SCORE2OP_2021] Yet the heart and brain are linked through shared haemodynamics, autonomic and neurohumoral signalling, and inflammation, and injury to one organ often accompanies injury to the other.[@Valenza_NatRevCardiol; @Scheitz_LancetNeurol_2018] Covert brain injury, including white matter hyperintensities, is itself associated with future stroke and dementia.[@Debette_JAMANeurol_2019; @Duering_STRIVE2_2023]

Large imaging studies have described this heart–brain axis. In UK Biobank, cardiac structure and function and aortic stiffness are associated with white matter hyperintensities, brain volumes, and cognition,[@Zhao_Science_2023; @McCracken_2022; @Masci_EHJ_2025] consistent with cohort evidence that arterial stiffening transmits pulsatile flow to the cerebral microvasculature[@Mitchell_Brain_2011; @Suri_PLoSMed_2020] and with the high burden of silent brain infarcts in atrial fibrillation.[@Conen_JACC_2019] Multi-organ imaging and proteomic signatures of biological ageing also predict chronic disease.[@Tian_NatMed_2023; @Oh_Nature_2023] However, these studies were largely cross-sectional and analysed cardiac and brain measures in parallel, so they could not show whether abnormality accumulates in a consistent order across the two organs. In addition, plasma proteomic risk models have added little beyond clinical risk factors for the prediction of cardiovascular events.[@Royer_EJPC_2024]

The order matters clinically. If cardiac abnormality consistently precedes brain injury, cardiac imaging could identify people at an earlier and potentially modifiable stage of heart–brain disease; if a stage that summarises this order predicts events beyond SCORE2, it could help to reclassify people whose risk is misestimated. Subtype and Stage Inference (SuStaIn) is an unsupervised method that infers, from cross-sectional data, both subgroups with distinct progression patterns and the sequence in which markers become abnormal.[@Young_2018; @Aksman_2021; @Young_NRN_2024] It has been applied mainly to neurodegenerative disease and, more recently, to chronic obstructive pulmonary disease,[@Young_COPD_2020] but not across the heart and brain.

We therefore applied SuStaIn to cardiac MRI, brain MRI, and plasma proteomic markers in UK Biobank to infer the sequence of heart–brain abnormality. We then tested whether the resulting stage was associated with cardiovascular and cerebrovascular events after imaging, whether it improved risk assessment beyond SCORE2, and whether these findings held in independent participants.

%% H1 Methods
%% H2 Study design and participants
UK Biobank recruited about 500 000 people aged 40–69 years in 2006–10; from 2014, a subset returned for multimodal imaging, including brain and cardiac MRI.[@Littlejohns_2020] Plasma proteins were measured in about 53 000 participants by the UK Biobank Pharma Proteomics Project with the Olink Explore 3072 platform.[@Sun_Nature_2023] Of 502 411 participants, 95 031 had at least one study marker, 27 744 had complete cardiac and brain MRI markers, and 3770 of these also had proteomic data; these 3770 participants formed the derivation sample (figure 1; appendix {app_flow}). The other 23 974 participants with complete MRI markers did not contribute to model fitting and formed the independent sample. UK Biobank has approval from the North West Multi-centre Research Ethics Committee (21/NW/0157), and all participants gave written informed consent. This research was done under UK Biobank application [AUTHOR ACTION: application number]. The study is reported according to the STROBE guideline, and the prediction analyses follow current guidance on the evaluation of clinical prediction models.[@Collins_2024_part1]

%% H2 Heart–brain markers
Ten markers were specified a priori to represent three systems. Three cardiac MRI domain scores represented left ventricular (LV) remodelling, LV myocardial dysfunction, and aortic stiffness, derived from 82 automatically extracted cardiac and aortic phenotypes.[@Bai_NatMed_2020] Four brain MRI scores represented hippocampal atrophy, cortical thinning, white matter hyperintensity (WMH) burden, and white-matter free water (isotropic volume fraction), derived from 587 imaging-derived phenotypes from the UK Biobank pipeline.[@AlfaroAlmagro_2021; @Duering_STRIVE2_2023] Multi-variable domain scores were the first principal component of their constituent phenotypes, with signs aligned so that higher values indicated greater abnormality; WMH burden was log-transformed total WMH volume adjusted for head size. Three plasma protein co-expression modules (M13, M20, and M5), identified by weighted correlation network analysis and associated with cardiac or brain MRI phenotypes in preceding analyses, were represented by their module eigengenes.

Each marker was expressed as a z score relative to a normative reference of participants free of cardiovascular, cerebrovascular, and neurological disease, using models conditional on age and sex (reference samples: proteomics 45 522, cardiac MRI 29 081, and brain MRI 39 155 participants).[@Bethlehem_Nature_2022] The z scores therefore express abnormality beyond that expected for age and sex (appendix {app_m2}).

%% H2 Subtype and Stage Inference
SuStaIn models each subtype as a sequence of events, each event being the point at which a marker reaches a z score threshold, and assigns each person a subtype and a stage, the number of events reached.[@Young_2018; @Aksman_2021] We used thresholds of z=1, 2, and 3 for each marker (appendix {app_m3}), giving 30 events in the main model (All-10). Two sensitivity models were fitted in the same participants: MRI markers plus the M13 module (24 events) and MRI markers only (18 events; aortic stiffness at z=1 only, because higher thresholds were rarely reached). Models with one to five subtypes were compared with the ten-fold cross-validation information criterion (CVIC), using 25 start points and 100 000 Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) iterations. Uncertainty in the sequence was summarised with positional variance diagrams from the MCMC samples. Because the number of events differs between models, stage was expressed as a percentage of the maximum stage and analysed per 10 percentage points, and in groups of 0, 1, 2, and 3 or higher.

To characterise the inferred order, we computed for each subtype the posterior probability, across MCMC samples, that the first cardiac event preceded the first brain event. We then classified every model stage by the organs it involved (none, heart only, brain only, or heart and brain). To check that the order did not simply reflect marker prevalence, we correlated the event order with the prevalence of each marker at z≥1. In the independent sample, the MRI-only model was applied without refitting by averaging over 1000 posterior sequence samples (appendix {app_m4}), and observed cardiac–brain co-abnormality at z≥1 and z≥2 was summarised with odds ratios (ORs). To test whether the sequence could be recovered without the derivation data, we also refitted the MRI-only model de novo, with two subtypes, in a random subsample of {rep_n} independent participants and compared the inferred sequences with those of the derivation models using Spearman correlation of posterior mean event positions.

To map the stage, the subtypes, and the coupling anatomically, we rebuilt normative z scores for {rs_n} regional measures ({rs_n_brain} brain and {rs_n_heart} cardiac, including wall thickness and strain in the 16 American Heart Association segments) and for global left ventricular measures; {rs_n_oom} of these, including wall thickness, subcortical volumes, and white-matter fractional anisotropy (FA) and mean diffusivity (MD), were not inputs to any domain score. We described regional z scores by subtype and stage group and compared the subtypes at stage 1 or higher with linear models adjusted for stage, age, and sex, assessing replication of the difference map between samples by Spearman correlation. In the independent sample, we also regressed each brain region on a standardised cardiac composite and on each cardiac domain score, and each cardiac measure on white-matter and grey-matter brain composites, adjusted for age and sex (and, in sensitivity analyses, vascular risk factors), with false discovery rate control; tissue classes were compared by median regional coefficients with participant-level bootstrap CIs (appendix {app_regm}).

%% H2 Outcomes
Outcomes were identified from hospital inpatient diagnoses (ICD-10; UK Biobank fields 41270 and 41280) and the underlying cause of death (field 40001; appendix {app_icd}). The primary outcome was a composite heart–brain vascular event, comprising stroke (I60, I61, I63, and I64), transient ischaemic attack (G45), ischaemic heart disease (I20–I25), heart failure (I50, I11.0, I13.0, and I13.2), and atrial fibrillation (I48). Secondary outcomes were major events (stroke, myocardial infarction [I21–I23], or heart failure), cerebrovascular events, cardiac events, the individual components, and cardiovascular death (underlying cause I00–I99). Depression, anxiety, Parkinson's disease, and chronic kidney disease were examined as exploratory, non-vascular comparators. Deaths with a matching underlying cause were counted as events.

In the derivation sample, follow-up began at the imaging visit. Participants with the outcome on or before imaging were excluded for that outcome, and follow-up ended at the event, death, or Sept 30, 2021 (the last date of complete hospital records), whichever came first. The same definitions were applied in the independent sample, with follow-up from each participant's imaging visit. As a sensitivity analysis, we repeated the independent-sample analysis with a common landmark (April 1, 2020) as time zero, excluding people with the outcome before the landmark.

%% H2 Statistical analysis
Associations were estimated with Cox proportional hazards models adjusted for age and sex (model 1) and additionally for systolic blood pressure, current smoking, diabetes, total and HDL cholesterol, BMI, and hypertension (model 2, primary). In the derivation sample, missing covariate values were imputed once by chained equations (appendix {app_m6}; missing data in appendix {app_miss}), with complete-case analysis as a sensitivity analysis; in the independent sample, model 2 was fitted in participants with complete covariates. Linear trend across stage groups was tested with the group coded ordinally, and proportional hazards were assessed with Schoenfeld residuals.

For incremental value, 10-year SCORE2 (age <70 years) or SCORE2-OP (age ≥70 years) risk was calculated for the UK low-risk region from age at imaging and recruitment risk factors (appendix {app_m5}).[@SCORE2_2021; @SCORE2OP_2021] Cox models containing the complementary log–log transformed SCORE2 risk, with and without stage, were compared by likelihood ratio tests and Harrell's C-index; 95% CIs and optimism correction for the C-index difference (ΔC) were obtained from 1000 bootstrap samples. Continuous net reclassification improvement (NRI) and integrated discrimination improvement were estimated at 3 years, and clinical utility was assessed by decision curve analysis at 3 years for threshold probabilities of 0·5–10%.[@Vickers_2006] Event rates were also tabulated by stage within ESC SCORE2 risk categories (low-to-moderate *vs* high or very high, using age-specific thresholds).[@Visseren_ESC_2021] To test whether the sequence-based stage added information beyond simpler summaries, we compared it with the number of abnormal markers (z≥1) and the mean marker z score in nested models.

Subtype differences were tested in stage-adjusted models with stage-by-subtype interaction terms. Sex was taken from UK Biobank records (field 31, recorded at recruitment and updated by self-report); gender was not assessed. Effect modification by sex was examined with a stage-by-sex interaction term and sex-specific models. Sensitivity analyses excluded the first year of follow-up, people with prevalent cardiovascular or peripheral arterial disease, or people with diabetes; used the posterior expected stage; were restricted to participants with high subtype certainty (posterior probability ≥0·60) or to those of self-reported White ethnicity (field 21000); adjusted for SCORE2 only; or added HbA1c, cystatin C, and Townsend deprivation index.

In the independent sample, we examined the association of stage with risk factors, with disease diagnosed on or before the imaging visit (logistic regression adjusted for age and sex), and with incident events after imaging (Cox model 2). In an organ-concordant analysis, we compared participants with isolated cardiac, isolated brain, or combined cardiac and brain abnormality (any marker z≥1) with those without abnormality, separately for disease before imaging and for incident events. The SCORE2 analyses were repeated in the independent sample; because of its size, 95% CIs for ΔC were obtained from 1000 bootstrap samples with model coefficients fixed at their full-sample estimates. Finally, we compared the discrimination of risk-factor models with the addition of stage, abnormal marker count, or mean z score.

Analyses used Python 3.12 with pySuStaIn 0.1, lifelines 0.30, statsmodels 0.15, and scikit-learn 1.9. Parts of the analysis and figure code were drafted with an AI agent and were reviewed and re-run by the authors (see declaration of generative AI). Tests were two-sided with α=0·05. The secondary and exploratory outcomes were not adjusted for multiple comparisons and should be interpreted accordingly.

%% H1 Discussion
In this study of UK Biobank participants who underwent cardiac and brain MRI, a data-driven model of heart–brain abnormality had three main findings. First, in every subtype and model, the first cardiac abnormality preceded the first brain abnormality, a de novo refit in independent participants reproduced the two subtypes and their order, the subtypes had distinct and replicated regional signatures, and cardiac abnormality co-occurred preferentially with white-matter injury. Second, the resulting heart–brain stage was associated with stroke, heart failure, atrial fibrillation, and cardiovascular death after imaging, independently of conventional risk factors, and this association was confirmed in nearly 24 000 independent participants in whom the model was applied without refitting. Third, the stage separated risk within SCORE2 categories and modestly improved discrimination, mainly by reclassifying people without events to lower risk.

Previous imaging studies have established that cardiac and brain measures are correlated.[@Zhao_Science_2023; @McCracken_2022; @Masci_EHJ_2025] Our findings add an order to these correlations. In every model, a cardiac event came first, and this precedence was not explained by marker prevalence or by the proteomic markers. This order is biologically plausible. LV remodelling and dysfunction reduce cardiac output and promote atrial fibrillation and embolism, and aortic stiffening exposes the cerebral microvasculature to pulsatile pressure,[@Mitchell_Brain_2011; @Suri_PLoSMed_2020; @Conen_JACC_2019] whereas the brain changes that followed—free water and WMH—are imaging markers of small vessel disease.[@Duering_STRIVE2_2023] The preferential coupling of cardiac abnormality with white-matter rather than hippocampal or cortical markers points to a vascular rather than a primarily neurodegenerative pathway. The regional analysis sharpens this picture: coupling was concentrated in deep periventricular tracts, typical sites of small vessel disease,[@Duering_STRIVE2_2023] and was carried by aortic stiffness and LV remodelling rather than by myocardial dysfunction, which is more consistent with transmission of pulsatile and pressure load to the brain than with low cardiac output.[@Mitchell_Brain_2011] The subtypes add an anatomical dimension: the remodelling-led subtype combined wall thickening and aortic stiffening with slightly more white-matter free water, whereas the dysfunction-led subtype showed more hippocampal, cortical, and subcortical involvement, a replicated pattern also present in measures not used to fit the model. Nevertheless, SuStaIn infers order from cross-sectional data, so the sequence is a probabilistic description of the population rather than proof that cardiac abnormality causes brain injury within individuals,[@Young_NRN_2024] and a heart-first order could also arise if cardiac MRI is more sensitive than brain MRI to early injury.

The organ-concordant analysis gives this order a clinical meaning. In the independent sample, isolated brain abnormality was associated mainly with cerebrovascular disease diagnosed before imaging, whereas cardiac abnormality, alone or with brain abnormality, was associated with stroke after imaging. Brain MRI abnormality may therefore partly record cerebrovascular injury that has already occurred, whereas cardiac abnormality marks an ongoing haemodynamic and embolic substrate for future events. This interpretation is consistent with the strongest prospective associations being with stroke, heart failure, and cardiovascular death, and with the concept that cardiac and cerebral injury interact bidirectionally.[@Scheitz_LancetNeurol_2018; @Valenza_NatRevCardiol] It suggests that brain MRI findings such as WMH should prompt cardiac assessment, and that cardiac MRI abnormalities should prompt attention to cerebrovascular prevention. These organ-specific findings, based on few events in the derivation sample, require confirmation.

From a clinical perspective, the stage was associated with events independently of the risk factors that make up SCORE2. Its incremental discrimination was modest (ΔC 0·031 in the derivation and 0·007 in the independent sample) and similar in size to that reported for plasma proteomic and other omic additions to clinical risk scores,[@Royer_EJPC_2024] and the reclassification improvement came mainly from people without events being moved to lower risk. Within the high or very high SCORE2 category, event rates at stage 3 or higher were more than twice those at stage 0, whereas people in the low-to-moderate category at stage 0 had the lowest rates. These gradients suggest that, where cardiac and brain MRI are already available, the stage could help to target intensified prevention and to avoid overtreatment. The net benefit in decision curve analysis was small, however, and confined to a narrow range of thresholds. In addition, the MRI-only model performed similarly to the full model, so proteomic data do not appear to be needed for prognostic use.

For discrimination, the stage did not outperform simple summaries of marker abnormality, such as the mean z score, so we do not propose it as a superior predictor. Its added value lies in interpretation: it places each person on an ordered heart–brain trajectory, indicates which organs are involved and, through the organ-concordant pattern, distinguishes accumulated brain injury from a cardiac substrate for future events. Multi-organ ageing clocks and proteomic organ-age signatures also capture cross-organ health,[@Tian_NatMed_2023; @Oh_Nature_2023] but they do not describe the order of involvement. Imaging is costly, so the stage is best suited to people who already undergo cardiac and brain MRI for clinical or research reasons, rather than to population screening.

Stage was also associated with depression and anxiety in the derivation sample, but not significantly with depression in the independent sample, and with neither Parkinson's disease nor chronic kidney disease in either sample. This specificity supports a vascular interpretation; the association with depression is compatible with a vascular contribution to late-life depression but could reflect shared risk factors or chance.

This study has several strengths. It modelled cardiac, brain, and proteomic markers jointly while propagating uncertainty in the sequence; it anchored follow-up at the imaging visit, excluded prevalent disease, and accounted for deaths; it confirmed the findings in a large independent sample both without refitting and with a de novo refit; and it compared the stage with simpler alternatives. The study also has limitations. First, follow-up after imaging was short, so the derivation sample had few events and wide CIs, particularly for stroke and cardiovascular death. Second, the sequence was inferred from cross-sectional data, and repeat imaging is needed to confirm within-person progression; it was also estimated in women and men together from sex-normalised markers, so sex-specific orderings were not examined. Third, the CVIC difference between two and three subtypes was small, subtype assignment was uncertain in the independent sample (median posterior probability {rs_psub_ind}), and the subtypes did not differ in risk, so we regard the stage as the robust prognostic quantity and the subtypes as anatomical phenotypes. The regional analyses were descriptive, and regional differences by stage and subtype are partly expected by construction, although measures not used to fit the model showed the same subtype pattern; the coupling analyses do not depend on the stage. Fourth, covariates, including blood pressure, and proteomic markers were measured at recruitment, a median of {gap_med} years before imaging, which may allow residual confounding. Fifth, outcomes were derived from hospital and death records, which under-ascertain conditions managed in primary care, such as atrial fibrillation and heart failure. Sixth, UK Biobank participants, particularly those who attend imaging, are healthier and less ethnically diverse than the general population,[@Fry_2017] so absolute risks and generalisability to other populations need to be established. Finally, the analyses of secondary and exploratory outcomes were not adjusted for multiplicity.

In conclusion, subclinical heart–brain abnormality appears to accumulate in a heart-first, white-matter-predominant order. A stage that summarises this order is associated with cardiovascular and cerebrovascular events beyond SCORE2 and separates risk within SCORE2 categories, although its gains in discrimination are modest. External validation with longer follow-up and repeat imaging is needed before the stage can inform clinical risk assessment in people undergoing cardiac and brain MRI.
